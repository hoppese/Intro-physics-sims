Circular Motion: what makes it go round
What this shows
  • Seven classic situations: a puck on a string, a car on a flat curve, a car on a banked curve, a conical pendulum, a vertical circle (a ball on a string, or a roller-coaster car in a perfectly circular loop), a Ferris wheel, and a satellite in orbit. In the loop the track pushes toward the centre and gravity changes the speed; on the Ferris wheel the speed is steady and the level seat pushes up and grips sideways.
  • Going round a circle at speed v takes an acceleration v²/r toward the centre. No special force does it: the real forces (tension, friction, the normal force, gravity) have parts toward the centre that add up to m v²/r.
  • When the forces cannot supply it, the motion changes: a cut string or a skidding car goes straight on along its velocity, not outward; a string or track at the top of a loop goes slack, and the object falls like a projectile. A satellite that is too slow or too fast for a circle goes into an ellipse (or crashes, or escapes).
  • The models are ideal: no air resistance, a massless string, a car treated as a point with all its weight on the road. On the big curves the car is drawn larger than life, or it would be a speck; so is the satellite. In orbit the clock runs fast (one circular orbit in about 12 s) and reads in minutes.
How to use
  • Choose a situation from the menu on the right and set its values with the sliders (or type them). Changing anything starts the motion again from the beginning.
  • ▶ Run (or Space) starts the clock; ↺ goes back; ⏭ steps; 1× switches to slow motion.
  • Drag the picture to turn it; 3D / Top / Side are set views; the wheel or −/+ zooms.
  • The table splits every force along r (toward the centre) and z (up; along the path, t, for a vertical circle), and sets ΣF_r beside m v²/r. Components and r, t, z axes draw the same split on the picture.
  • Hide forces takes away the forces, the acceleration and their values, so students draw the free-body diagram first.
