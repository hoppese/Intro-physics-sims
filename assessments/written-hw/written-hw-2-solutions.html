Written HW 2 — Full Solutions

Problem statements from Knight, Physics for Scientists and Engineers (5th ed., Global Edition), Chapter 22. K = 8.99×109 N·m²/C² and g = 9.8 m/s² throughout.

22.17 (3 pts)

Three charges lie on the x-axis (Figure EX22.17): qA = +1.0 nC at x = 0, qB = −1.0 nC at x = 1.0 cm, and qC = +4.0 nC at x = 2.0 cm. What is the magnitude of the net force on charge A?

MODEL: Treat A, B, and C as point charges. The net force on A is the vector sum of the force from B alone and the force from C alone.
VISUALIZE: B is negative, so it attracts A — that force pulls A to the right, toward B (+x̂). C is positive, so it repels A — that force pushes A to the left, away from C (−x̂). The two forces point in opposite directions.
SOLVE: By Coulomb's law,
FB on A = K|qAqB|/rAB² = (8.99×10⁹ N·m²/C²)(1.0×10⁻⁹ C)(1.0×10⁻⁹ C)/(0.010 m)² = 8.99×10⁻⁵ N, direction +x̂
FC on A = K|qAqC|/rAC² = (8.99×10⁹)(1.0×10⁻⁹)(4.0×10⁻⁹)/(0.020 m)² = 8.99×10⁻⁵ N, direction −x̂
These are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction, so
Fnet,A = (8.99×10⁻⁵ N)x̂ + (8.99×10⁻⁵ N)(−x̂) = 0 N
REVIEW: This cancellation isn't an accident — C sits twice as far from A as B does, but carries four times the charge, and Coulomb's law goes as (charge)/(distance)². Doubling the distance cuts the force by 4×, and quadrupling the charge multiplies it by 4×, so the two effects exactly offset. A good example of how sensitive the inverse-square law is to distance.
22.18 (3 pts)

Three charges lie on a vertical line (Figure EX22.18): qA = +1.0 nC at the top, qB = −2.0 nC located 2.0 cm below A, and qC = +2.0 nC located 1.0 cm below B. What is the magnitude of the net force on charge B?

MODEL: Treat A, B, and C as point charges.
SOLVE: A is positive and B is negative, so A attracts B upward, toward A:
FA on B = K|qAqB|/rAB² = (8.99×10⁹)(1.0×10⁻⁹)(2.0×10⁻⁹)/(0.020 m)² = 4.5×10⁻⁵ N, direction +ĵ (up)
C is positive and B is negative, so C attracts B downward, toward C:
FC on B = K|qCqB|/rCB² = (8.99×10⁹)(2.0×10⁻⁹)(2.0×10⁻⁹)/(0.010 m)² = 3.6×10⁻⁴ N, direction −ĵ (down)
Adding these as vectors,
Fnet,B = (4.5×10⁻⁵ N)ĵ − (3.6×10⁻⁴ N)ĵ = −3.1×10⁻⁴ N ĵ, so the magnitude is 3.1×10⁻⁴ N (downward)
REVIEW: C is closer to B than A is (1.0 cm vs. 2.0 cm) and carries twice A's charge, so it makes sense that C's pull wins decisively — unlike Problem 22.17, nothing here is set up to cancel.
22.19 (3 pts)

Three charges sit at the corners of an equilateral triangle with 1.0 cm sides (Figure EX22.19): qA = +2.0 nC at the top vertex, qB = −2.0 nC at the bottom-left vertex, and qC = +1.0 nC at the bottom-right vertex. What is the force F on the 1.0 nC charge? Give your answer as a magnitude and direction.

MODEL: Treat all three as point charges. Charge C feels two forces — a repulsion from A and an attraction to B — that must be added as vectors.
VISUALIZE: Put B at the origin and C at (1.0 cm, 0), so A sits at (0.5 cm, 0.87 cm), completing the equilateral triangle. A and C are both positive, so A repels C straight along the line from A through C. B and C are opposite in sign, so B attracts C straight along the line from C to B.
SOLVE: Both pairs are separated by the same 1.0 cm side, and both charge products have the same magnitude (2.0 nC × 1.0 nC), so the two individual forces come out equal:
F = KqAqC/r² = (8.99×10⁹)(2.0×10⁻⁹)(1.0×10⁻⁹)/(0.010 m)² = 1.80×10⁻⁴ N
The unit vector from A to C is (0.50, −0.87), so the repulsion from A contributes
FA on C = (1.80×10⁻⁴ N)(0.50, −0.87) = (9.0×10⁻⁵, −1.56×10⁻⁴) N
The unit vector from C to B is (−1, 0), so the attraction to B contributes
FB on C = (1.80×10⁻⁴ N)(−1, 0) = (−1.80×10⁻⁴, 0) N
Adding components,
Fnet,C = (−9.0×10⁻⁵, −1.56×10⁻⁴) N  ⇒  magnitude = √[(9.0×10⁻⁵)² + (1.56×10⁻⁴)²] = 1.8×10⁻⁴ N
Both components are negative, so F points into the third quadrant, at tan⁻¹(1.56/0.90) = 60° below the −x-axis — i.e., straight out through C, away from the midpoint of edge AB.
REVIEW: Because the two individual forces are equal in magnitude and 120° apart, the resultant must bisect that angle — which is exactly what the components give. That the two forces come out equal at all is itself a direct consequence of qAqC and |qB|qC being the same product (2.0 nC²) at the same 1.0 cm separation.
22.33 (2 pts)

A +12 nC charge is located at the origin. What are the electric fields at the positions (x, y) = (5.0 cm, 0.0 cm), (−5.0 cm, 5.0 cm), and (−5.0 cm, −5.0 cm)? Write each electric field vector in component form.

MODEL: The field of a positive point charge points radially outward from it, with magnitude E = Kq/r².
SOLVE: At (5.0 cm, 0): r = 5.0 cm, straight along +x̂.
E = (8.99×10⁹)(12×10⁻⁹)/(0.050 m)² = 4.3×10⁴ N/C  ⇒  E = (4.3×10⁴, 0) N/C
At (−5.0 cm, 5.0 cm): r = √50 cm = 7.07 cm, direction (−1/√2, 1/√2).
E = (8.99×10⁹)(12×10⁻⁹)/(0.0707 m)² = 2.16×10⁴ N/C  ⇒  E = (−1.5×10⁴, 1.5×10⁴) N/C
At (−5.0 cm, −5.0 cm): same radius as the point above, direction (−1/√2, −1/√2), so
E = (−1.5×10⁴, −1.5×10⁴) N/C
REVIEW: The last two points are mirror images of each other across the x-axis, at the same distance from the charge, so their field magnitudes should match — they do (2.16×10⁴ N/C both) — and only the sign of the y-component flips, exactly as the geometry demands.
22.34 (4 pts) BIO

A 0.10 g honeybee acquires a charge of +23 pC while flying.

a. The earth's electric field near the surface is typically 100 N/C, downward. What is the ratio of the electric force on the bee to the bee's weight?

b. What electric field (strength and direction) would allow the bee to hang suspended in the air?

MODEL: Treat the bee as a point charge in a uniform field. Its weight is Fg = mg; the electric force on it is Fe = qE.
SOLVE: (a) The ratio of the two forces is
Fe/Fg = qE/mg = (23×10⁻¹² C)(100 N/C) / [(0.10×10⁻³ kg)(9.8 m/s²)] = 2.3×10⁻⁶
(b) For the electric force to balance gravity, qE = mg, so
E = mg/q = (0.10×10⁻³ kg)(9.8 m/s²) / (23×10⁻¹² C) = 4.3×10⁵ N/C, directed upward (so the force on the positive charge points up, opposing gravity).
REVIEW: Part (a)'s ratio is minuscule — Earth's ordinary fair-weather field is millions of times too weak to matter to a flying bee. Part (b)'s answer, 4.3×10⁵ N/C, is roughly 1000× the field strength that causes dielectric breakdown (sparking) in air, a reminder that electrically suspending a bee like this isn't something you could actually set up in a room, even though the algebra is clean.
22.36 (3 pts)

A +5.0 nC charge sits at the origin (Figure EX22.36). Point 1 is at (x, y) = (1.0 cm, 2.0 cm), point 2 is at (1.0 cm, 0 cm), and point 3 is at (1.0 cm, −2.0 cm). What are the electric fields at points 1, 2, and 3? Give your answer in component form.

MODEL: The field of a positive point charge points radially outward from it, with magnitude E = Kq/r².
SOLVE: At point 2: r = 1.0 cm, straight along +x̂.
E = (8.99×10⁹)(5.0×10⁻⁹)/(0.010 m)² = 4.5×10⁵ N/C  ⇒  E₂ = (4.5×10⁵, 0) N/C
Points 1 and 3 are both r = √5 cm = 2.24 cm from the charge, with direction (1/√5, ±2/√5).
E = (8.99×10⁹)(5.0×10⁻⁹)/(0.0224 m)² = 8.99×10⁴ N/C
E₁ = (4.0×10⁴, 8.0×10⁴) N/C    E₃ = (4.0×10⁴, −8.0×10⁴) N/C
REVIEW: Point 2 is closer to the charge (1.0 cm) than points 1 and 3 (2.24 cm), so it should have the larger field — and by a factor of about (2.24)² ≈ 5, exactly what the numbers show (4.5×10⁵ vs. 8.99×10⁴ N/C). Points 1 and 3 are mirror images across the x-axis, so their fields are mirror images too, differing only in the sign of the y-component.
