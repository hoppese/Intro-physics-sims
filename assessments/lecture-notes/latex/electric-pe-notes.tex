\noteshead{Electric Potential Energy}{}

\goal{Understand where electric potential energy comes from --- summing over
pairs of point charges, or pushing a charge against a field --- and use it to
find the potential energy of an electric dipole in a uniform field. Along the
way: a system can have \emph{negative} PE, and the configuration you call
``zero'' is not always the lowest one available.}

%============================================================
\nsec{Why ``zero'' isn't always the lowest}

We usually call the ground $h=0$ and say a ball resting on it has zero
gravitational PE. Now picture a hole in the ground, right under the ball.

\vspace{6cm}

\checkpoint{If the ball falls into the hole, is its PE above or below the
zero you originally chose? What does that tell you about calling a PE
``zero''?}

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=9pt,parsep=4pt,topsep=4pt]
\item Opposite charges ($+$ and $-$): $U$ is \bl[2.4cm]\ at any finite
separation, and $U\to0$ only as $r\to\infty$. So $U=0$ is actually the
\bl[3.2cm]\ value here --- pulling the charges closer always finds a
\emph{lower} (more negative) PE, like digging the hole deeper.
\item Like charges (same sign): $U$ is \bl[2.4cm]\ at any finite separation,
and again $U\to0$ as $r\to\infty$. Here $U=0$ \emph{is} the \bl[3.2cm]\
value --- you can't push like charges to a lower PE than infinitely far
apart.
\end{itemize}

%============================================================
\nsec{Potential energy from pushing against a field}

A uniform field $\vec E$ pushes a positive charge $q$ with force $qE$. Push
the charge a distance $d$ the \emph{opposite} way --- straight against the
field.

\eqline{$\Delta U = $}

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=9pt,parsep=4pt,topsep=4pt]
\item This is exactly the ball-and-hole idea: you do \bl[2.4cm]\ work
pushing against the field, and that work becomes stored PE.
\item Let the charge go, and it moves \emph{with} the field instead: now
$\Delta U$ is \bl[2.4cm] --- the field does the work, and spends the stored
PE.
\end{itemize}

\checkpoint{A positive charge is released and moves along $\vec E$. Does its
PE increase or decrease? Where did the energy go?}

\vspace{5cm}

%============================================================
\nsec{The dipole in a field}

A dipole is a $+q$ and a $-q$ separated by $\vec d$ (dipole moment
$\vec p = q\vec d$), sitting in a uniform field $\vec E$, tilted at angle
$\theta$ between $\vec p$ and $\vec E$. Each charge sits at a different
place along $\vec E$ --- use \S3's pushing idea on each charge, then add.

\vspace{7cm}

\eqline{$U(\theta) = $}

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=9pt,parsep=4pt,topsep=4pt]
\item At $\theta=90^\circ$ (perpendicular to the field): $U=$ \bl[2.4cm].
\item At $\theta=0^\circ$ (aligned with the field): $U=$ \bl[3.2cm] ---
which is \bl[2.4cm]\ than the $\theta=90^\circ$ value!
\item At $\theta=180^\circ$ (anti-aligned): $U=$ \bl[3.2cm].
\end{itemize}

\checkpoint{Which orientation is the most stable (lowest PE)? Is that the
orientation where $U=0$? What does this have in common with the ball and
the hole?}
