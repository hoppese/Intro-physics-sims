%============================================================
\nsec{Why a symmetry argument is needed}

Gauss's law, $\displaystyle\oint \vec{E}\cdot d\vec{A} = \frac{q_{\text{enc}}}{\varepsilon_0}$,
is true for \emph{every} closed surface. But it only lets you \emph{solve} for $E$ when you
can pull $E$ outside the integral. That needs two things to be true everywhere on the
surface you choose:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=2mm, topsep=1mm]
  \item $E$ has the same \bl[3cm]\ everywhere on it, and
  \item $\vec{E}$ is everywhere either \bl[2.6cm]\ or \bl[2.6cm]\ to the surface.
\end{enumerate}

When both hold, the flux integral collapses to a single product: \qquad$\Phi = {}$\bl[3.5cm]

\checkpoint{Does the surface have to match the shape of the \emph{charge}, or the shape of
the \emph{field}? Why does a cube fail for a point charge?}

\nsec{Infinite line of charge}

Linear charge density $\lambda$. $\vec{E}$ points radially out from the wire and cannot
depend on position along it, so the Gaussian surface is a \emph{coaxial cylinder} of
radius $r$ and length $L$.

\nsec{Infinite sheet of charge}

Surface charge density $\eta$. $\vec{E}$ points straight away from the sheet on both
sides, so the Gaussian surface is a \emph{pillbox} straddling it, with end caps of
area $A_{\text{cap}}$.

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[x=0.9cm,y=0.9cm]
  % --- the sheet, edge on. Thin line so the + signs above it stay legible.
  \draw[red!70!black, line width=0.8pt] (-3.1,0) -- (3.1,0);
  \foreach \x in {-2.7,-1.8,-0.9,0,0.9,1.8,2.7}{
    \node[red!70!black, scale=0.62] at (\x,0.24) {$+$};}
  \node[red!70!black, scale=0.8] at (-3.4,0) {$\infty$};
  \node[red!70!black, scale=0.8] at (3.4,0) {$\infty$};

  \draw[gray!55, dashed] (-1.15,-0.9) rectangle (1.15,0.9);
  \draw[gray!55, dashed] (0,0.9) ellipse (1.15 and 0.28);
  \draw[gray!55, dashed] (0,-0.9) ellipse (1.15 and 0.28);
