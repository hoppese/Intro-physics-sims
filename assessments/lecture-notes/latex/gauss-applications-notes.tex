\goal{State Gauss's law and understand what it says, then use it to \emph{compute}
$\vec{E}$ --- not just check it --- for the distributions where symmetry makes that

%============================================================
\nsec{What Gauss's law says}

\textbf{a) Flux.} The number of electric field lines that exit or enter a \bl[2.6cm]\ is
the same, no matter the \bl[2.2cm]\ of the surface. By ``number of field lines'' we mean
\emph{flux}:
\[
\Phi = {}\bl[6.5cm]
\]
Notes: the surface's area vector is \bl[2.0cm]\ to the surface and points \bl[1.6cm], by
convention. If lines are exiting, $\Phi$ is \bl[1.5cm]; if entering, $\Phi$ is \bl[1.5cm].
A line that just passes through counts $+1$ for leaving and $-1$ for entering, so it
contributes \bl[1.2cm]\ net.

\textbf{b) Relation to charge.} The number of field lines --- also called \bl[2.2cm] ---
through a closed surface is equal to \bl[3.0cm]. Does it matter how far from the charge the surface
is? \bl[1.3cm] The closed surface we integrate over is called a \bl[2.6cm].

\checkpointsp{A point charge sits at the center of a Gaussian sphere. If you double the
sphere's radius, does $\Phi$ change? Does $E$ at the surface change? (Different questions!)}{2}

%============================================================
\nsec{Using it: match the surface to the symmetry}

\textbf{a)} Use Gauss's law when the charge distribution has one of the three classical
symmetries: \bl[2.2cm], \bl[2.2cm], \bl[2.2cm].

\textit{Definition of symmetry:} \lines{2}

\textbf{b)} Choose a Gaussian surface that respects the \bl[2.0cm]\ of the charge
distribution. If the distribution is spherically symmetric, make your surface spherically
symmetric too, \bl[3.2cm]\ (similarly for cylindrical and planar symmetry).

If you've done this, $\vec E$ will be everywhere either \bl[2.2cm]\ or \bl[2.2cm]\ to your
Gaussian surface, and $\Phi=E\oint dA=EA$ --- Gauss's law becomes plain algebra:
$EA=q_{\text{enc}}/\varepsilon_0$.

\checkpointsp{Why does a cube fail as a Gaussian surface for a single point charge?}{2}

%============================================================
\nsec{Warm-up: a point charge, then a shell}

\derivbox{3.8cm}{Sketch a point charge $+q$ with a Gaussian sphere of radius $r$ around it.}

By symmetry $\vec E$ is radial and constant in magnitude on the sphere, so
$EA=q_{\text{enc}}/\varepsilon_0$ with $A=4\pi r^2$ and $q_{\text{enc}}=q$ gives
$E={}$\bl[2.6cm] --- Coulomb's law, recovered from Gauss's law.

Now make it a thin \textbf{shell} of charge $Q$, radius $R$, instead of a point.
\textbf{Outside} ($r>R$): the Gaussian sphere encloses \bl[1.5cm], so
$E={}$\bl[2.6cm] (identical to a point charge --- the shell might as well not have a
size). \textbf{Inside} ($r<R$): the Gaussian sphere encloses \bl[1.5cm], so
$E={}$\bl[1.5cm].

\checkpointsp{The sphere you'll work below is \emph{solid}, not a shell --- charge fills
the whole volume. Will its field inside behave the same way as the shell's?}{2}

\nsec{Infinite line of charge}

Linear charge density $\lambda$. $\vec{E}$ points radially out from the wire and cannot
depend on position along it, so the Gaussian surface is a \emph{coaxial cylinder} of
radius $r$ and length $L$.

\emph{two} directions, $x$ and $y$, and $\vec{E}$ points straight away from it along $\pm z$
on both sides.
