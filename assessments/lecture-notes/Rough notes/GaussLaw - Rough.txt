Gauss's Law

1. What does it say?


a) Information on flux:
The number of electric lines that exit/enter a *closed surface* is the same, regardless of the *size/shape* of the surface.

By "number of field lines" we mean flux:

*\Phi = \oint \vec E dot d \vec A = same thing with cosine explicit*

notes:

- surface area vector is *perpendicular* to the surface, and points out, by convention.
- So, if lines are exiting the surface, flux is *positive*. If they are leaving the flux is *negative*
- Every time a line leaves it counts as +1, and every time it enters it is -1. So, a line that passes through will be *-1 + 1 = 0*


b) Relation of flux to charge:
The number of field lines (AKA *net flux*) is equal to *q_enc / eps_0*

So, from "counting field lines" (i.e. computing the net flux through a surface), we can know how much *charge* there is inside.

Does it matter how far away from the charge we are? *No!*

Formally, we count the field lines by performing the surface integral (it's the same one you wrong above). 

*\Phi = \oint \vec E dot d \vec A*

The closed area over which we integrate is called a *Gaussian surface*


2. How is it useful, in a practical sense?

a) Use it when you have a charge distribution in one of the 3 classical symmetries:
	- *Spherical*
	- *Cylindrical*
	- *Planar*

Definition of Symmetry: *If you close your eyes and I change the system in a symmetric way, when you open your eyes, you can't tell*

b) Define a gaussian surface that respects the *symmetry* of the charge distribution
	- If the charge distribution is spherically symmetric, define your surface to be spherically symmetric,
			*centered on the charge distribution*
	- ... similarly for cylindrical and planar symmetries


If you've done this, the electric field through your surface will be perpendicular to your Gaussian surface, and the integral will be easy. (Use the language you already about about perp & parallel)
